# Why trials don't turn into deals, and why "more fun" doesn't sell

As of 2026-10-28. For the founder and the founder's dad; internal. **This is not a step report.** It saves, at the founder's request, Claude's chat answers of 2026-10-28 to: "Why would prospects who took the trial not buy, when the game is clearly faster and less boring than traditional assessments? HR aren't the ones taking it, the candidates are, so why should they care? Why can't we sell even one assessment after Telkom, when the report even links personality to role fit? And if companies say they already have an assessment, how do the big Indonesian players sell to them?" Corrections 1 (Agate is DEUS's game developer and DEUS is one of its five Next Chapter partners) and 6 (nobody on the team is a psychologist) are applied. Where this differs from the Step 8 verdict, the verdict is the considered call.

## The answer

**The trial is where the buyer tests DEUS's one promise, a better candidate experience, and in the personality game that promise fails; once it fails, nothing else in the offer beats the test they already have.** The game is not faster than the test it is compared with, it is a reading task rather than a game, and HR, not the candidate, pays for a long test in drop-outs, complaints and slower hiring. Where candidates must finish (as at Telkom), enjoyment is worth nothing to the buyer; where they don't have to, the game is too long for them to finish. Either way "more engaging than traditional" is not what wins the deal. The established players don't beat "we already have one": they *are* the test companies already have, or they offer the same step online, small enough to try, signed by a psychologist and approvable by procurement (interpretation).

## 2. Why the trial loses the deal (interpretation)

1. **It is not faster than what buyers compare it with.** A standard personality questionnaire such as DISC or a Big Five form usually takes about 10–20 minutes (ASSUMPTION: general knowledge, not in the files). DEUS's personality game takes 60 minutes or more, so about 3–6 times longer (derived). The 3 h 20 min suite is about as long as a classic paper battery (IST about 90 min, Pauli about 60 min, plus personality and drawing tests) (Benchmarks, section 3; [W23, W31]). "Faster" holds, at best, against the whole old battery, never against the short test the buyer has in mind.
2. **It is not less boring, only boring in a different way.** A questionnaire is dull but short and predictable: the candidate can see the end. An hour of story text, in small type on a phone, with no sense of progress, is reading with extra clicks rather than play. The word "game" also raises the expectation: a form never promised fun, so it cannot disappoint; a "game" that turns out to be reading does.
3. **HR pays for a long test, not the candidate** (ASSUMPTION: general hiring practice):
   - candidates drop out, and the best ones, who have other offers, drop first;
   - complaints ("the text is too small", "it froze", "how long is this?") land on HR;
   - hiring slows, and HR is judged on filling roles quickly with good people;
   - the company's name is on the experience, not DEUS's.
4. **The HR head plays the trial on behalf of their candidates.** If a motivated person who is being pitched finds it long and boring, they reasonably expect tired applicants on mobile data to find it worse.
5. **The trial is a sample of DEUS.** Small text and poor mobile support make a buyer wonder about what they cannot see: the scoring, the support, how candidate data is handled.
6. **The trial tests DEUS's one promise.** Before it, the buyer is buying "engaging, candidates enjoy it"; the trial is the only moment they check it themselves. When it fails, what is left is a new vendor with no psychologist, an unvalidated personality game, the same or more candidate time, and a price between bands (Benchmarks, sections 3 and 5). "We'll think about it" is then a polite no.

## 3. Captive and non-captive candidates: "more fun" has no buyer (interpretation)

The founder's point about Telkom, that candidates had to finish or be disqualified, splits buyers in two:

| Buyer | Does length cause drop-outs? | What the game's fun is worth | What decides the deal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Candidates have no choice:** state companies, big brands, MT intakes with hundreds of applicants per opening | No: candidates sit through anything | Almost nothing; nobody pays extra for it | A psychologist behind the result, validation, price, procurement (Benchmarks, section 2). DEUS is weak on all four |
| **Candidates have options:** F&B and retail frontline, scarce skills, experienced hires | Yes: an hour of reading on a phone loses applicants | Would matter, but the game is too long to deliver it | Experience and speed, which the trial shows as "long, small text". This is where DEUS's outreach went (F&B: 0 deals) |

So the pitch "more engaging than traditional" fails in both groups. Even Telkom did not buy fun: its Assessment Center was already looking for a gamification vendor, came through Agate, and had its own psychologists to stand behind the results (Recap, Phase 7; Correction 2; Benchmarks, section 4).

Telkom's near-full completion is therefore **not evidence** that candidates liked the game.

## 4. Why the role-fit report doesn't rescue the deal (interpretation)

- A personality description is information; **"this candidate fits this role" is a hiring decision.** When HR rejects someone on it, they must be able to say what it rests on.
- Today that is a personality game with no validation, multiplied by weightings DEUS set itself, with no psychologist on the team (Fact sheet, Validation; Correction 6). A careful HR head reads a confident score on an unknown base as risk, not value.
- In Indonesia, recommendation-style verdicts come from psychologists: tenders name psychologists in the required team [W24, W25], and a bureau's price includes a psychologist's report (Benchmarks, sections 2–3). **The stronger the conclusion, the more it needs a psychologist and validation behind it.**
- The trial itself gives reasons to doubt the scoring. Characters praise some answers, which teaches the "right" ones. Visual Perception showed 25 after 100% accuracy on two minigames. The top trait ("Innovative") didn't match a cautious, plan-first answer pattern (Playtest, section 7; one run, so a sanity check rather than proof). An HR head who sees this has every reason to distrust a role-fit verdict built on it.
- Most prospects never reach the report: the hour-long game comes first and loses them there.
- Building it fits a pattern already named: when sales lagged, DEUS added more (Step 11, reason 10).

With a partner psychologist who reviews the weightings and signs the report, the same feature becomes a reason to buy, at the bureau price band (Benchmarks, recommendations 3 and 6).

## 5. Why nothing sold after Telkom (interpretation; detail in Benchmarks, section 4)

| Telkom's Assessment Center had | Prospects since have |
|---|---|
| An open search: it was looking for a gamified assessment (Recap, Phase 7) | No search: they already have an assessment they're content with (Fact sheet, Funnel) |
| A route through Agate, the vendor it asked (Correction 2) | DEUS's own outreach, which has closed no deal (Step 11, reason 3) |
| Its own psychologists and assessors [W10, W11] | A need for DEUS to supply the credibility DEUS doesn't have |

No one has been hired whose job is to sell (Step 11, reason 5). In one line: **DEUS sells a hiring decision without what buyers need to trust one, to buyers who aren't looking, through a route that has never closed a deal.**

## 6. How the big players sell to companies that "already have one" (facts with interpretation)

Client counts below are the companies' own claims.

1. **They got the slot early and grew with it.** Talentlytica grew out of an HR consulting business founded in 2007 and went from 108+ clients in 2021 to 460+, hiring account managers [W1, W4]. Telkom's Assessment Center began as Telkom's own unit in 1990; PPM dates from 1967 [W10, W18]. Once a company picks a provider it rarely switches (ASSUMPTION): the same inertia DEUS meets works for them.
2. **They replace the same step, not add one.** Hiring already includes a standard battery (IST, Kraepelin, DISC and so on) [W23]. Talentlytica's PAULINE is the Pauli test online [W7]: "the test you trust, online, faster" needs no new justification to managers.
3. **A psychologist and evidence stand behind the result.** Talentlytica's CEO is a psychologist designing tests since 1988; it publishes validation papers and Indonesian norms and holds ISO/IEC 27001 [W1, W3]. Bureaus' reports are psychologist-signed.
4. **Starting is tiny, so nobody has to switch.** Ikutpsikotes sells 5 tests for Rp799,000 [W8], within an HR officer's own authority (ASSUMPTION). A company keeps its old provider and tries the new one on one role. Ikutpsikotes claims 900+ companies, about 280 test-takers each (derived). DEUS's minimum is Rp10 juta, after a meeting and a proposal (Fact sheet, Pricing).
5. **Procurement can approve them:** approved-vendor registration, the documents tenders ask for, past similar contracts [W24–W26].
6. **Others sell for them:** Talentlytica says 70+ partners resell its tests under their own names [W2].
7. **They arrive when the need changes:** a hiring wave, a transformation programme (Talentlytica and Pos Indonesia [W5]), a cheating scandal (stand-ins in the 2023 joint state-company recruitment [W28]), a new rule. At those moments "we already have one" stops being true.

"We already have one" is a real answer only when the offer asks the buyer to **add** something. Each of the seven has a matching recommendation for DEUS in Benchmarks (3, 5, 6, 7 and 11).

## 7. The belief to test

Across all four questions the same belief came back: DEUS's game is obviously better, so buyers who say no must be getting it wrong. The only evidence that it is better comes from inside DEUS, and DEUS rarely uses Discover on its own candidates (Founder answers). Since Telkom, buyers have answered almost every time with silence; that is their answer, not a misunderstanding. The Rp1 billion spent on the first game (Fact sheet; Recap, Phase 1) is not a reason to keep defending it (CLAUDE.md, rule 3). This belief should be tested before anything else is built, because every channel and feature since Telkom rests on it.

---

## Recommendation

In order. Items 1 and 5–6 repeat Benchmarks; the others are new.

1. **Keep DEUS's own personality game out of trials and proposals** (Benchmarks, recommendation 1).
2. **Time the trial as a candidate would, against a real competitor.** Buy Ikutpsikotes' 5-test package (Rp799,000 [W8]) and take one of its tests on a cheap Android phone over mobile data with a stopwatch; do the same with DEUS's game and with Next Chapter's personality and cognitive modules. Write down minutes, text size and where attention drops. Cost: under Rp1 juta. *DEUS's game is half done (2026-10-29): the desktop playtest estimates 60–80 minutes. A clocked run on a cheap Android phone is still needed, and so is the competitor's test.*
3. **Don't lead with Next Chapter's personality module until it has been timed.** The plans make it the lead offer on the strength of "about 18–20 minutes" (Agate deck); the founder's experience of Level Up's module is "very time-consuming". Confirm with Agate which module is which, and use it in trials only if item 2 shows it is clearly shorter than the buyer's current personality test.
4. **Ask the prospects who went quiet, in one line:** "I assume the timing isn't right, and that's fine. Could you tell me the main reason? One line is enough." Write the exact words down. Longer conversations use `outputs/06c-interview-guide.md`.
5. **Ask Telkom's Assessment Center for the data that would persuade a buyer:** average time per module, how many candidates asked for help or complained and about what, and whether people DEUS ranked highly stayed and performed (Benchmarks, recommendation 8).
6. **Get a partner psychologist to review the role-fit weightings and sign the report** (Benchmarks, recommendation 3).
7. **Show the role-fit report, without the game, to three HR heads** and ask: "Would you reject a candidate based on this? What would you need to see before you would?"
8. **Change the pitch** from "more fun than traditional" to "shorter than the step you run now, results in one day, signed by a psychologist" (Benchmarks, recommendations 4–5).

## Key findings

1. **The trial loses the deal because it tests DEUS's only promise, and the personality game fails it:** 60+ minutes of reading against a 10–20-minute questionnaire (ASSUMPTION for the questionnaire), with small text on mobile.
2. **HR, not the candidate, pays for a long test** in drop-outs, complaints and slower hiring, so the HR head judges the trial on behalf of hundreds of applicants.
3. **"More fun" has no buyer:** where candidates must finish, as at Telkom, fun is worth nothing; where they needn't, the game is too long to finish.
4. **The role-fit report raises the bar instead of clearing it:** a hiring recommendation from an unvalidated game and DEUS's own weightings needs a psychologist behind it before buyers can act on it.
5. **The big players don't beat "we already have one"; they are it,** or they offer the same step online, small enough to try, psychologist-signed and procurable.
